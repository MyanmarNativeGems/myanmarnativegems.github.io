Spinel spent centuries being mistaken for ruby. Several famous "rubies" in royal regalia, including the Black Prince's Ruby in the British Imperial State Crown, are in fact red spinels. Modern gemology separates the two easily, and spinel is now collected enthusiastically in its own right.

## Color

Red and pink spinels are the classics, colored by chromium like ruby. The finest reds approach ruby in intensity. Spinel also occurs in violet, blue, lavender, and gray tones; cobalt-bearing blue spinel is particularly rare. Because spinel is singly refractive, its color often reads unusually pure and crisp to the eye.

## Durability

Spinel measures 8 on the Mohs hardness scale. That is slightly softer than corundum but still comfortably durable for regular wear.

## Clarity and cut

Fine spinels are often eye-clean, so visible inclusions matter in valuation. Good cutting shows off the stone's characteristic brilliance and color purity.

## Treatments

A notable attraction of spinel is that, as a general market matter, treated stones are less prevalent than in the ruby and sapphire trades. This is a general statement about the material, not a claim about any particular stone: treatment status should always be confirmed per stone, and we encourage you to ask.

## Before you buy

Ask about weight, color origin, treatment status, and any documentation. Spinel remains attractively priced relative to comparable ruby, which is part of why collectors value it.
